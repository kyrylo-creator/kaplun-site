**What stress is**

* Stress is a normal, adaptive response to change and challenge.
* The “fight/flight/freeze” shorthand describes automatic threat responses: the sympathetic nervous system ramps up and hormones like adrenaline, noradrenaline, and cortisol rise to mobilize the body.
* Recovery requires the parasympathetic “rest-and-digest” mode. Resilience depends on healthy balance between these systems.

**When stress becomes a problem**

* Brief stress is expected; prolonged or intense stress (chronic stress) can erode energy, sleep, focus, mood, and immunity.
* Common signs: shallow rapid breathing, elevated heart rate, muscle tension, sweating, mental fog, sleep and appetite changes, fatigue.
* High stress levels are linked with worse trajectories across many conditions; individual outcomes vary with genetics, environment, and lifestyle.

**Quick self-check**

* Breathing and pulse are immediate markers.
* Behavior: irritability and urgency often indicate stress; interest in rest, connection, and nourishment points to recovery.

**What helps**

* Breathing and relaxation: slow diaphragmatic breathing (about 4–6 breaths/min), progressive muscle relaxation, mindfulness/meditation.
* Movement: regular moderate activity (walking, yoga, tai chi, swimming, mobility work).
* Sleep: 7–9 hours, dark/cool/quiet room, consistent schedule, fewer evening screens. 
* Support: social connection and sharing feelings with trusted people.
* Psychotherapy: build skills to respond differently to stressors and restore balance. 
* Everyday structure: realistic planning, prioritization, boundaries, regular meals, and limiting stimulants later in the day.

**Practices and cautions**

* Integrated mind–body systems (yoga, qigong, body-oriented methods) combine breath, gentle movement, and interoceptive awareness—useful for regulation.
* Avoid extremes and miracle claims; add tools within a coherent plan guided by a professional.

**When to seek help**

* If stress disrupts sleep, work, or relationships; if panic-like symptoms or hopelessness recur; or if you can’t “switch off,” consult a psychologist/psychotherapist.
* In acute crises, seek psychiatric or emergency support first.

**How therapy helps**

* Assess stress load and maintaining factors.
* Teach self-regulation skills (breathwork, relaxation, mindfulness).
* Reshape thinking patterns and responses.
* Build sustainable recovery routines and a maintenance plan.
* Coordinate with medical care when needed.
