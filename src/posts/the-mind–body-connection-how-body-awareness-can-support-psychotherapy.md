The ability to notice and understand the signals coming from our own bodies is an important part of psychological well-being. It can help us recognise emotions, become more aware of our stress responses, and gradually develop better self-regulation skills.

In psychology, this process is known as **interoception** — the ability to sense, notice, and interpret internal bodily signals, such as heartbeat, breathing, muscle tension, hunger, fullness, warmth, and cold.

The importance of being connected to the body has been clear to me for a long time. It made sense intuitively and was supported by my practical experience. There is also a growing body of scientific research exploring the relationship between interoception, emotional regulation, and psychotherapy outcomes.

People who are more aware of their bodily signals may be better able to recognise emotional responses, understand their internal state, and use this awareness to regulate their reactions.

However, it is important to remember that feeling something in the body does not always mean interpreting it correctly. Bodily signals can be ambiguous, and paying excessive attention to them can sometimes increase anxiety.

## Why Do I Keep Asking About Bodily Sensations?

There are a few simple questions I regularly ask clients during psychological consultations and yoga sessions: How are you feeling right now? What are you experiencing at this moment? What sensations do you notice in your body? What emotions are present right now?

These questions may seem very simple. Yet answering them is not always easy.

## How Can Body Awareness Support Psychological Work?

### 1. Recognising Emotions Earlier

Emotional experiences are often accompanied by physiological changes. Our breathing, heartbeat, muscle tension, and body temperature may change. As we gradually learn to notice our own bodily signals, we may become more aware of what is happening before the emotional response becomes overwhelming. This creates more space to pause, identify the emotion, and choose how to respond rather than reacting automatically.

### 2. Noticing What Is Difficult to Put into Words

During talk therapy, people sometimes move towards analysis, explanations, and intellectualisation. This can be a useful way of making sense of experience, but sometimes it can also distance us from the immediate emotional experience. Questions about the body can help bring attention back to what is happening in the present moment.

### 3. Gradually Reconnecting with Yourself After Trauma

Following traumatic experiences, some people may experience a sense of disconnection from their bodies, emotional numbness, derealisation, or dissociation. It may become difficult to understand what they are feeling, recognise their own needs, or feel fully present in the moment. In such situations, gentle work with body awareness may become part of the process of rebuilding a sense of connection with oneself and the surrounding environment.

### 4. Developing Emotional Self-Regulation

When we understand our internal state better, it may become easier to notice when tension is increasing and an emotional response is becoming more intense. This does not mean that emotions should be suppressed or immediately eliminated. On the contrary, the aim is to learn to notice them, tolerate their presence, and gradually choose an appropriate response.

## How Can We Develop Body Awareness?

There are different ways to practise body awareness. Personally, I use practices from yoga and Daoist movement systems. Below are a few simple exercises that can be adapted to individual needs.

### 1. Body Scan

Find a comfortable position, either sitting or lying down. You may close your eyes if you wish, but this is not necessary. Slowly move your attention from the top of your head down towards your feet. Notice what you experience in different parts of your body: tension, warmth, cold, tingling, heaviness, or an absence of any noticeable sensation. Do not try to relax or change anything. The task is simply to notice. For example: “My jaw feels tight”, “My shoulders are slightly raised”, or “I can feel warmth in my feet”. If the practice becomes uncomfortable, you can stop or shift your attention towards external objects.

### 2. The 5–4–3–2–1 Grounding Technique

This exercise helps shift attention away from anxious thoughts and towards the present moment through the senses. Slowly identify: 5 things you can see; 4 things you can touch; 3 sounds you can hear; 2 things you can smell; 1 thing you can taste. There is no need to do this perfectly or quickly. The important thing is to calmly notice what is happening around you.

### 3. Focusing on Points of Support

Sit on a chair or stand in a comfortable position. Bring your attention to the contact between your feet and the floor, your back and the chair, and your body and the surface supporting you. Notice how the surface supports your body, where you feel pressure, and where you experience a sense of ease. You can slightly change your position and compare the sensations. This simple exercise helps you become more aware of physical support and your body's position in space.

### 4. Gentle Breathing with a Slightly Longer Exhale

Sit comfortably. Take a calm breath in through your nose, followed by a slightly slower and longer exhale. If it feels comfortable, you can try inhaling for approximately four counts and exhaling for six. There is no need to hold your breath or force yourself to take a deep breath. For some people, a slightly longer exhale can reduce the subjective feeling of tension and help settle the breathing rhythm. However, this is not a universal technique. If you experience dizziness, breathlessness, or increased anxiety, return to your natural breathing pattern.

## An Important Note: Body-Based Practices Should Feel Safe

Not everyone feels comfortable focusing on internal bodily sensations. This is particularly relevant for people with traumatic experiences, panic symptoms, or significant anxiety. It is important not to force yourself to continue an exercise when it causes discomfort. You can keep your eyes open, shorten the practice, shift your attention towards your surroundings, or stop altogether. If body-based exercises trigger strong reactions, it may be helpful to discuss them with a psychologist or psychotherapist and find an approach that suits your needs. Body awareness does not replace psychotherapy, but it can be a useful complement to it.

## Reconnecting with Yourself Begins with Attention

Sometimes all it takes is a few seconds to pause and ask yourself:

“What am I feeling right now? How am I experiencing this moment? What is happening in my body?”

These are simple questions, but they can help us gradually develop greater awareness of our internal state.

**When we become more aware of our body's signals, it may become easier to understand our emotions, needs, and reactions. And that is an important part of psychological work and self-care.**
