Today, we hear words like “spirituality,” “energy,” “mindfulness,” “enlightenment,” “transformation,” “retreats,” and “spiritual practices” more and more often.

They have become part of contemporary culture. We see them on social media, in advertising, and in different courses and training programmes. We are encouraged to transform ourselves, change our energy, find inner harmony, and reach a new level of consciousness.

But what do these words actually mean?

## Are People Becoming More Spiritual?

It is difficult to give a simple answer.

One of the problems is that many of the concepts used today in the field of spiritual development do not have a clear or universally accepted definition.

What exactly does “spiritual transformation” mean? How can it be measured? How do we know that someone has become more spiritual? What criteria can help us distinguish actual changes from a subjective feeling of change?

There are no simple answers to many of these questions. There are also no universally accepted scientific criteria for measuring such phenomena.

This does not, in itself, mean that a person's spiritual experience has no value.

Personal experiences, the search for meaning, and a sense of connection with other people or with nature can be deeply meaningful.

At the same time, I think it is important to distinguish between **personal experience and claims presented as objective facts**.

## When Spirituality Becomes Marketing

Any popular concept eventually becomes part of marketing.

Words such as “transformation,” “energy,” “mindfulness,” and “enlightenment” can be used to describe completely different things.

Today, one particular practice may be presented as a universal path to happiness. Tomorrow, another one becomes popular. Trends change, and so do the keywords used in advertising.

This is why I am cautious about bold promises of rapid personal transformation.

## I Prefer Concrete Words

Personally, I try to use these broad, universal terms as little as possible.

Not because I think spirituality, meditation, retreats, or different practices are inherently bad.

On the contrary, certain practices can be genuinely helpful for a particular person.

But a word gradually loses its meaning when it is used to describe almost everything.

I feel much more comfortable with concrete concepts such as **stress, anxiety, emotions, the body, behaviour, relationships, needs, values, personal boundaries, meaning in life, and psychological well-being**.

When we use more specific language, it becomes easier to understand what we are actually working with and how we might evaluate change.

Perhaps that could also be called transformation.

But I don't really need the word.

**Sometimes, fewer big words and more real substance are enough.**
